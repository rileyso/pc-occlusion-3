# Waymo difficulty baseline — text for the report

## Hypotheses (stated before running the evaluation)

The Waymo Open Dataset defines detection difficulty from LiDAR observation richness: labels without any LiDAR points are ignored, LEVEL_2 comprises labels with at most five LiDAR points in the box (plus a labeler-flagged-hard criterion that does not transfer to KITTI), and LEVEL_1 comprises the rest, with LEVEL_2 metrics reported cumulatively [Sun et al., CVPR 2020, §5.1]. Transplanting this rule onto the KITTI validation pedestrians, the baseline is expected to prove four things:

1. **H1 — sensor-relative degeneracy.** The 5-point threshold is calibrated to Waymo's LiDAR, not to the task: on KITTI's denser HDL-64E scans almost no pedestrian (≈1%) falls below it, so the literal rule is nearly vacuous on this benchmark — the LEVEL_2 subset is too small to estimate stably, the cumulative LEVEL_2 AP coincides with the LEVEL_1 AP, and the binary label carries almost no information about detection outcomes. Benchmark difficulty annotations are properties of the sensor as much as of the scene, and do not transfer across LiDARs.
2. **H2 — directional validity where the rule bites.** The heuristic is not wrong, merely sensor-specific: at a threshold rescaled to the host LiDAR (≤ 26 points ≈ the 10th-percentile pedestrian count), LEVEL_2 pedestrians will be detected far less often and at far lower precision than LEVEL_1 pedestrians, for every detector. Observation richness does rank difficulty; the rule encodes it coarsely.
3. **H3 — coarseness.** Detection probability is a continuous, monotone function of point count, so collapsing it into one bit discards most of the difficulty signal: a large detection-rate gradient will remain *inside* LEVEL_1 (across point-count terciles), and the binary label will predict detection status substantially worse than the continuous log point count.
4. **H4 — range confounding.** Point count falls with range, so LEVEL_2 membership will largely re-encode distance (median range of LEVEL_2 objects far above LEVEL_1); the rule cannot distinguish a distant pedestrian from a close but occluded one at equal point count — exactly the limitation a geometry-based visibility metric addresses.

Expected verdict: the Waymo difficulty heuristic is *effective* only in a weak sense — a valid, coarse, sensor-relative ranking of observation richness that conflates range with visibility. Together with the DCD baseline (which showed a published geometric similarity metric behaving as a return-density proxy), it establishes that neither the benchmark annotation nor the surveyed geometric metric isolates occlusion at comparable range and density, motivating the thesis visibility metric.

## Methodology

**Role of the baseline.** The thesis research question asks whether a fine-grained, geometry-based measure of partial occlusion explains detection difficulty better than the coarse difficulty annotations used by established benchmarks, of which the Waymo Level rule is the point-count representative. The DCD baseline (§"Density-Aware Chamfer Distance Baseline") tested the geometric-similarity representative and found it behaving as a return-density proxy; the present baseline completes the pair by testing the coarse-annotation representative on identical data, protocol and detectors, so that the thesis visibility metric is later compared against both on matched footing.

**The rule and what transfers.** The baseline transplants Waymo's published difficulty assignment, not Waymo's AP/APH metric. Following Sun et al. (CVPR 2020, §5.1), 3D labels without any LiDAR points are ignored; a label is LEVEL_2 if it is flagged hard by a labeler *or* has at most five LiDAR points in its box; the remaining labels are LEVEL_1; and LEVEL_2 metrics are reported cumulatively (LEVEL_1 included, §3.3). Two adaptations were required for KITTI. First, the labeler-flag criterion has no KITTI equivalent and is dropped, so the transplant carries only the point-count criterion (KITTI's own Easy/Moderate/Hard labels play the labeler role and remain available for a future cross-benchmark comparison). Second, Waymo's AP/APH — heading-weighted, recall-normalised, with class-specific IoU thresholds — is replaced by the thesis's matching protocol (below) so that all baselines, bins and figures in the thesis remain mutually comparable.

**Transplant design and threshold selection.** The rule was evaluated in two arms. The *literal arm* applies the published ≤ 5-point threshold unchanged, giving the honest answer to "what does the Waymo annotation mean on this sensor?". Because the point-count distribution showed the literal threshold to be degenerate on KITTI (see Results), a *density-matched arm* re-tunes the same one-bit rule to the host sensor: the threshold was fixed at 26 points — the 10th percentile of the KITTI pedestrian point-count distribution — selected from the point-count quantiles alone, before any detection outcome was computed, so the operating point is not tuned to the result. A threshold sweep (5 / 10 / 26 / 50 / 100 points; the first threshold whose LEVEL_2 subset exceeds the 55-instance stability minimum, the density-matched point, and two progressively more balanced cuts) then maps the stratification gap as a function of where the bit is placed, covering the trade-off between group balance and group difficulty. No threshold beyond 5 was tuned per detector; level assignments are detector-independent by construction.

**Data.** The natural pedestrian benchmark: 824 of the 3,769 KITTI validation frames contain at least one pedestrian, yielding 2,280 ground-truth pedestrians (2,279 evaluable; one object has no returns and is ignored per the Waymo zero-point convention). Point counts — LiDAR returns inside the ground-truth box, the same quantity Waymo thresholds — come from the DCD-baseline pipeline and were verified identical to the benchmark's own `num_points_in_gt` on every row. Detector predictions are the pedestrian-preserving validation outputs of the four OpenPCDet detectors used throughout the thesis (PointPillars, SECOND, PointRCNN, PV-RCNN), reused unchanged.

**Evaluation protocol.** Greedy confidence-ranked matching of predictions to ground truth at 3D IoU ≥ 0.50 with a confidence threshold of 0.1; predictions matching other annotated objects are ignored rather than counted as false positives (Gilroy semantics, identical to the occlusion-bin and DCD-bin tables). Per level: 3D mAP@[0.50:0.95], AP@IoU 0.50, true-positive (detection) rate, false positives per ground truth and false-negative rate. Exclusive LEVEL_1 and LEVEL_2 subsets are evaluated for the difficulty gap; the cumulative LEVEL_2 variant (all non-ignored objects) is evaluated alongside because that is what Waymo's protocol actually reports. Subsets below the 55-instance stability minimum are flagged and interpreted only anecdotally.

**Effectiveness quantification.** "Effectiveness" of the heuristic is operationalised four ways: (i) the stratification gap — LEVEL_1 minus LEVEL_2 detection rate and AP, per detector and per sweep threshold; (ii) discrimination — the area under the ROC curve (AUC) of detection status predicted by the binary level, compared with continuous log point count and with ground range as covariates; (iii) the hidden gradient — detection-rate spread across point-count terciles *within* LEVEL_1, the signal the one-bit label discards; and (iv) confounding — the correlation of LEVEL_2 membership with ground range and the range distributions of the two levels. Where continuous curves are shown, P(detected) is modelled by logistic regression cubic in standardised log point count with 95% bootstrap confidence bands (200 resamples, seed 0).

## Analysis

The literal rule behaves as hypothesised. Only 20 of 2,279 pedestrians (0.9%) have five or fewer returns, so LEVEL_2 metrics rest on a sample far below any stable-estimate minimum: AP@IoU 0.50 within LEVEL_2 is 0.3% (PointPillars), 0.0% (SECOND), 0.0% (PointRCNN) and 0.3% (PV-RCNN) against 41.1%, 37.7%, 23.6% and 44.6% within LEVEL_1 — an enormous but anecdotal gap — while the cumulative LEVEL_2 metrics that Waymo's protocol actually reports coincide with the LEVEL_1 values to within half a point. The binary label's association with detection outcomes is correspondingly vacuous: AUC 0.51 for every detector, statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip (H1 confirmed). Waymo's difficulty annotation is a property of its sensor suite as much as of its scenes: transplanted across LiDARs, "LEVEL_2" does not denote a comparable difficulty stratum.

Where the rule is given a threshold matched to the host sensor, it behaves as H2 predicts. At the 26-point cut (LEVEL_2 = 243 pedestrians, 10.7%), LEVEL_2 AP@IoU 0.50 falls to 1.2%, 1.5%, 0.2% and 3.2% versus 45.0%, 41.1%, 26.4% and 48.3% in LEVEL_1, and detection rates fall by 50.6, 48.1, 42.0 and 51.7 percentage points respectively; the LEVEL_1 − LEVEL_2 gap is positive for every detector at every sweep threshold. Observation richness ranks difficulty exactly as the Waymo heuristic assumes.

The rule is nevertheless coarse, as H3 predicts. Detection probability climbs continuously and monotonically across two orders of magnitude of point count, and one bit of it discards most of the signal: inside literal LEVEL_1 alone, detection rates span the point-count terciles (edges at 71 and 231 points) by 33.9 pp (PointPillars), 34.6 (SECOND), 64.4 (PointRCNN) and 32.4 (PV-RCNN) — a within-level gradient comparable to the between-level gap the rule does capture. Quantitatively, the binary level predicts detection with AUC 0.58–0.62 even at the density-matched cut, whereas continuous log point count reaches 0.71, 0.71, 0.82 and 0.72; ground range alone (0.64–0.78) also outperforms the transplanted label. A detector-independent difficulty annotation that a continuous covariate beats by 10–24 points of AUC is a lossy summary of the phenomenon it is meant to grade.

Finally, the rule is range-confounded, as H4 predicts: LEVEL_2 membership correlates with ground range at r = +0.64, and the LEVEL_2 range distribution is shifted wholesale upward (median 36 m versus 13 m for LEVEL_1; 54 m versus 15 m under the literal rule). A close, heavily occluded pedestrian and a distant, fully visible one enter the same stratum whenever their return counts agree — the rule measures *how much* was observed, never *why*. Combined with the DCD baseline's finding that the surveyed geometric alternative degenerates into a return-density proxy (r = +0.63 with point count), the conclusion is that neither the benchmark annotation nor the geometric metric separates visibility from range and density at matched operating conditions. This is the gap the thesis's ray-traced visibility metric is designed to close, and the present baseline supplies the coarse-annotation anchor for that comparison.

## Limitations

1. **One dataset, one class, one sensor.** The baseline transplants the Waymo rule *to* KITTI pedestrians; it never measures Waymo-side point counts (no Waymo ground truth was available locally), so the claim is a demonstrated one-way transfer failure, not a two-sensor comparison. The degeneracy direction is consistent with Waymo's own leaderboard reporting non-trivial LEVEL_2 subsets, but the exact density ratio between the sensors is not established here. Results are pedestrian-only; the rule would need re-evaluation for vehicles (whose boxes sample differently) and for other LiDARs.
2. **The density-matched threshold is a construct.** Waymo's paper publishes no LEVEL_2 prevalence, so "matching Waymo's operating point" is not literally possible; the 10th-percentile cut is defensible and was fixed from the point-count distribution alone, but it is one of several reasonable choices. The sweep mitigates this by showing the gap is positive at every threshold, yet no single density-matched number should be quoted as "the" Waymo-equivalent level.
3. **Literal LEVEL_2 is anecdotal.** n = 20 is far below the 55-instance stability minimum; the 0–0.3% AP values carry wide uncertainty and are reported only to show the rule's degeneracy, never as performance estimates.
4. **Point count is a blunt observation proxy.** Returns inside a bounding box include multipath and noise returns and, occasionally, returns from occluders that intersect the box volume; and the count conflates occlusion with range, object size, pose and sensor sampling pattern. This is precisely the confound under study, but it means the baseline can quantify the confounding, not remove it.
5. **Protocol differences from Waymo's own evaluation.** The thesis matching protocol (IoU 0.50 for pedestrians, score ≥ 0.1, Gilroy ignore semantics, AP over 101 recall levels) is deliberately not Waymo's APH; absolute numbers are therefore not comparable with Waymo benchmark tables, only internally across thesis baselines. The labeler-flag arm of the rule is untestable on KITTI.
6. **Operating point and metric choices.** The 0.1 score threshold and IoU 0.50 primary metric follow the thesis convention; stricter IoU (mAP@[0.50:0.95] is also reported) and other score thresholds would shift rates, though the monotone point-count relationship underlying all four hypotheses is threshold-independent.
7. **Statistical scope.** Per-level AP gaps are reported without confidence intervals (the bootstrap is applied to the continuous curves); with 2,279 objects the large gaps are clearly robust, but fine-grained sweep comparisons near zero would not be. The cubic-logistic curve fits extrapolate below ≈15 points where data is sparse and the left tail is model-driven (the 5th-percentile pedestrian has 16 points).
8. **Natural benchmark confounding.** The natural benchmark is unlabelled-occluder-confounded by construction (as documented for the Gilroy replication): the baseline measures how well the rule tracks detection difficulty, not the causal effect of occlusion — that remains the role of the thesis's controlled experiment and metric validation.

## How this baseline addresses the thesis

The thesis claims that coarse benchmark difficulty annotations are insufficient surrogates for occlusion difficulty, and proposes a fine-grained, geometry-based visibility score to replace them. For that claim to stand, the strongest coarse annotation available must first be measured on its own terms: how much difficulty signal does it carry, and where does it fail? This baseline supplies that measurement for the point-count representative, the difficulty annotation used by the most widely followed 3D detection benchmark.

Each finding maps onto one axis along which the thesis metric must improve. The degeneracy result (H1) establishes **non-portability**: a threshold fixed in points is a property of a sensor, so any annotation defined by raw counts must be re-derived per dataset — whereas the thesis metric is defined as a ratio of visible to observable surface area by ray tracing from the LiDAR origin, dimensionless and sensor-independent by construction. The coarseness result (H3) establishes **resolution**: one bit discards a continuous, monotone difficulty signal that log point count recovers with 10–24 points of additional AUC — the thesis metric is likewise continuous at the object level (v_i ∈ [0, 1]), preserving exactly the within-level ordering that the binary level collapses. The confound result (H4) establishes **attribution**: because point count falls with range (r = −0.61), the rule cannot say whether a poorly observed pedestrian is far or occluded; the thesis metric is designed to separate the two, external occlusion being measurable at fixed range, with range and point count retained as covariates rather than absorbed into the label. H2 matters as much as the failures: the rule's directional validity confirms that observation richness is the right *quantity* to grade, so a metric that grades it more finely, more portably and more interpretably is improving on a real signal, not inventing one.

Within the validation plan (§5.6), the baseline leaves reusable infrastructure: `per_object_waymo_levels.csv` carries, for every pedestrian, the level assignments, point count, range and per-detector detection status, so the planned logistic regressions can be run with the Waymo level as a covariate alongside the thesis metric v_i, DCD, range and density — the direct test of whether v_i explains detection status *beyond* the established annotation. Together with the DCD baseline, the two literature representatives are now measured on identical data and protocol: the geometric one fails by pointing in the wrong direction (density proxy), the annotation one fails by being too coarse, sensor-bound and range-confounded; the thesis metric is positioned precisely in the gap between them.
